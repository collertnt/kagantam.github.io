Digital differential manometer

A low-cost instrument that measures ±2 kPa, shows the reading on a built-in screen, and streams data to Python for logging and analysis.

    Define

    Students have no cheap way to log small pressure differences

    Liquid U-tube manometers are cheap and accurate, but they can't record data, they're read by eye (±1 mm at best), and they can spill. Commercial digital manometers log data but cost $150 to $500+ and are closed boxes you can't learn from.

    The need: a low-cost, open, digital instrument that measures differential pressure in the ±2 kPa range, shows it live, and logs it to a computer for analysis in Python.

    Who it's for

    • The design team: a buildable project that teaches instrumentation and coding.
    • Lab instructors and TAs: something safe, robust and repeatable for a teaching lab.
    • Future students: clear readings and a simple data file they can analyze.

    Started from a broad idea, narrowed it to one measurable quantity, then wrote the need as a function ("measure and log ΔP"), not a solution ("build an Arduino gadget").

    Research

    Three pieces of theory drive the whole design

    • Fluid statics, for calibration. Pressure under a column of water rises linearly with depth: about 97.9 Pa for every centimetre. A ruler and a cylinder of water make a free reference standard.
    • Piezoresistive sensing, for the electronics. Pressure bends a thin silicon diaphragm and changes its resistance. The MPXV7002DP outputs 0.5 to 4.5 V for −2 to +2 kPa, with 2.5 V at zero.
    • The Venturi effect, for the application test. Air speeds up through a narrow throat and its pressure drops, so a measured ΔP gives the flow rate.

    Brainstorm

    Three ways to sense pressure

    A. MPXV7002DP + Uno

    One analog differential sensor with two ports, read by the Arduino's ADC.

    For
    Made for this range. True differential. Simple wiring.
    Against
    10-bit ADC limits resolution. ±2.5% raw accuracy.

    B. Two BMP280s, subtracted

    Two absolute sensors, each reading about 101 kPa. Subtract to get ΔP.

    For
    Digital I2C. Very cheap.
    Against
    Subtracting two big numbers amplifies their errors.

    C. HX710B module

    A bridge sensor with a 24-bit ADC, about 0 to 40 kPa.

    For
    High resolution.
    Against
    One port only. Range far too wide for small ΔP.

    Display and port options were listed separately: a 16×2 LCD, a small OLED or laptop-only for the display; barbed bulkheads, push-to-connect fittings or a grommet for the ports.

    Select

    Concept A wins, 4.40 out of 5

    The weights were agreed before scoring, so they couldn't be bent toward a favourite. Accuracy got the highest weight because it is the requirement the whole calibration study tests.

    Sub-system choices

    • Display: 16×2 I2C LCD. Bigger characters than a small OLED, easier to read across a bench, and only 4 wires. Laptop-only fails R6.
    • Ports: barbed brass bulkheads. Push-to-connect looked more professional but needed reducers and extra tube stubs, which add leak points.

    Develop

    Pressure becomes a voltage, then counts, then logged data

    The circuit needs only nine connections and no extra resistors. The button uses the Uno's internal pull-up, and the LCD backpack and sensor breakout carry their own support parts.

    The Venturi test section

    To give the manometer a real job, it will measure air flow through a 3D-printed Venturi: one part, 70.5 mm long, with a 4.8 mm bore that narrows to a 2 mm throat. Drag the flow rate to see what the instrument should read.

    Sized the throat by working backwards: wanted ΔP to be at least 10× the accuracy target at a typical pump flow.

    Firmware and software decisions

    • The Arduino never waits for the laptop. It sends a line every 100 ms whether anyone is listening or not, so the display works with no computer attached.
    • Averaging 64 samples cuts random noise by about 8× and takes only about 7 ms.
    • Timing with millis() instead of delay() keeps the button responsive between readings.
    • Tare stores an offset rather than assuming 2.5 V is zero, which removes the sensor's offset error.
    • Python skips any line that isn't three numbers, so a glitch can't crash the logger.

    Buildplanned

    Six stages, with a test at the end of each

    Testing after every stage means a fault can only come from the stage just finished.

    1. Breadboard the sensor (about 1 h). Check the raw reading sits near 512 and moves the right way on each port.
    2. Add the LCD and tare button (about 1.5 h). Check the screen shows a value near 0 Pa and the button re-zeros it.
    3. Python data logger (about 2 h). Check a live plot appears and a CSV is saved.
    4. Calibrate on the breadboard (about 2 h). A baseline to compare with after assembly.
    5. Enclosure assembly (about 4 h). Drill, mount, solder, label. Re-run three calibration points.
    6. Venturi test rig (about 3 h). Print the Venturi, drill the throat and taps to size, connect the pump.

    The reference has its own error. Reading water depth to ±1 mm is already ±9.8 Pa, so if the combined uncertainty lands close to ±15 Pa, I can't claim better accuracy than the reference allows. The report will say so.

    Then the real job: measuring flow

    Air from an aquarium pump goes through a buffer bottle, then the Venturi, then into an upturned bottle of water. Timing how long it takes to collect 500 mL gives an independent flow rate to check against. At 1 to 4 L/min the predicted ΔP is 16 to 262 Pa.

    Textbook Venturis have a discharge coefficient of 0.95 to 0.99, but those values are for turbulent flow. This throat runs at a Reynolds number of only about 700 to 2,800, so I expect a lower number that changes with flow. Plotting it against Reynolds number is the result I'm most curious about.

    Where it could go next

    • A 16-bit external ADC to improve resolution about 16×.
    • A wider-range sensor for pipe-friction experiments.
    • A temperature sensor inside the box to correct drift automatically.
    • Using the ΔP signal as feedback to control pump speed: a first PID loop.
    • Wireless logging with an ESP32.
